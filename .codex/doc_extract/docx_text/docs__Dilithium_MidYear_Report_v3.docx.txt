Abstract

Post-quantum cryptography is becoming central to long-term digital security, because the rise of quantum computing undermines the public-key schemes, RSA and ECC, that protect today's systems. CRYSTALS-Dilithium, standardised by NIST as the Module-Lattice Digital Signature Algorithm in FIPS 204, is a lattice-based signature scheme that offers strong security at practical key and signature sizes. Its heavy polynomial arithmetic and its constant use of the SHAKE hash function, however, make a software-only implementation expensive on embedded platforms.

This thesis develops a modular, register-transfer-level hardware implementation of Dilithium, accelerating its two busiest primitives: the Keccak/SHAKE hashing engine and the Number-Theoretic-Transform engine. Each is written in SystemVerilog, checked against a SystemVerilog golden model inside a UVM environment with every transaction compared, and synthesised on an Intel Cyclone V FPGA. At this mid-year stage both modules are complete and fully verified. The Keccak engine supports SHAKE128 and SHAKE256, scales across four lanes at a measured 3.84 times, closes functional coverage over 208 tests, and reaches 84.31 MHz. The NTT engine performs the forward and inverse transforms and point-wise multiplication with a 2x2 conflict-free butterfly tile and a q-specific shift-add reduction, closes coverage over 53 tests, and reaches 75.44 MHz on only six DSP blocks at a leading cycle count of about 299. The results show that a carefully verified Cyclone V implementation is competitive on fabric-neutral measures, and they set a dependable base for the remaining work: the sampler, the controller and the full integration.

Chapter 1

Introduction

Background Information

Almost every secure digital service in use today leans on public-key cryptography. Authentication, secure boot, firmware checks and encrypted messaging all depend on it, and in practice that means RSA or Elliptic-Curve Cryptography (ECC). The safety of both rests on a single assumption: that factoring large integers, or solving the discrete-logarithm problem, is hopelessly slow on an ordinary computer [1].

Quantum computing breaks that assumption. Shor's algorithm, given a large enough quantum machine, factors integers and solves discrete logarithms in polynomial time, which would leave RSA and ECC effectively unprotected [1], [2]. The danger is not only a future one. An adversary can record encrypted traffic now and decrypt it later, once suitable hardware exists, so sensitive data with a long shelf life is already at risk. That store-now, break-later problem is what makes the move to quantum-safe cryptography pressing rather than academic.

To prepare for this, the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) ran a multi-round competition to choose and standardise post-quantum algorithms [2]. CRYSTALS-Dilithium, a lattice-based signature scheme, came through that process and is now published as the Module-Lattice Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA) in FIPS 204 [3], [5]. It pairs strong security arguments with reasonable key and signature sizes, which is why it was selected over several competing constructions.

Dilithium is not cheap to run, however. Key generation, signing and verification all revolve around polynomial arithmetic in the ring R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^256 + 1) and around heavy use of the SHAKE extendable-output function. On a small embedded processor those operations are slow and power-hungry. A dedicated hardware accelerator is the obvious answer, and building and verifying one is what this thesis sets out to do.

Significance and Motivation

The value of this work sits where two pressures meet. The shift to post-quantum cryptography is unavoidable, and at the same time more and more long-lived embedded and IoT devices are being deployed that must stay secure for ten years or more. Those devices are the least able to spare the processing budget that Dilithium demands in software, yet they are squarely in the path of the store-now, break-later threat. A compact, well-organised hardware implementation speaks directly to that need.

It also helps that the cost of Dilithium is not spread evenly. Measurements of existing implementations put roughly three-quarters of the run time in the signing phase, and most of that is taken up by the polynomial multiplications inside the rejection-sampling loop, each of which is an NTT. SHAKE is the other hot spot: at the top security level, building the public matrix A on its own needs 56 separate SHAKE128 runs. Because the work concentrates in these two primitives, hashing and the NTT, they are the sensible places to spend hardware effort, and they are what this report covers.

Figure 1 shows where the two engines sit inside the wider accelerator. Several Keccak cores run in parallel and feed a rejection sampler; the sampler produces coefficients in bursts, which a small FIFO smooths out before they reach the NTT engine at a steady rate.

Benefits of a Hardware Realisation

Moving these primitives into hardware buys three things. Throughput improves, because butterflies and hash lanes can run in parallel and be pipelined. Energy per operation drops, which matters most on battery-powered nodes. And the result is a verified, reusable block of IP on which a full ML-DSA accelerator, and later any side-channel protection, can be built. The order of priorities in this thesis is deliberate: get each module correct and well tested first, then make it fast, so that what is delivered can be trusted rather than merely measured.

Aims and Objectives

The aim of the thesis is to design and verify a modular, resource-efficient hardware implementation of the CRYSTALS-Dilithium (ML-DSA) signature scheme at register-transfer level. The objectives that follow from this aim are listed below.

Study the Dilithium reference C model and break the algorithm into blocks that map well onto hardware.

Design synthesisable SystemVerilog for the two most demanding primitives, the Keccak/SHAKE hashing engine and the Number-Theoretic-Transform engine.

Verify each block against a trusted golden reference inside a UVM environment, with full functional coverage and every transaction checked.

Synthesise each block on an Intel Cyclone V FPGA and measure its area, maximum clock frequency and latency.

Compare the measurements with the published literature and map out the route towards a complete, integrated accelerator.

Methodology

The work proceeds one module at a time. For each block the steps are the same: read the reference model, settle the architecture, write the RTL, verify it against a golden model, then synthesise and tune. A single rule runs through all of it, which is to keep correctness and speed apart. A module is first brought up in a plainly correct form and checked on every transaction; only after that is it optimised for area and frequency, and it is re-checked after each change. The repository keeps one module per Git branch so each engine can be finished and verified on its own before anything is integrated. Figure 2 gives the overall flow.

Chapter 2

Literature Review

Introduction

This chapter gathers the background and the prior work that frame the thesis. It begins with why post-quantum cryptography is needed and how ML-DSA is built, then turns to published hardware for the Keccak/SHAKE function and the Number-Theoretic Transform. The aim is to place the present work against what already exists: to show which design choices are settled practice, which trade-offs are still open, and where a Cyclone V implementation can contribute something defensible.

Post-Quantum Cryptography and ML-DSA

Of the various quantum-resistant families, lattice-based cryptography has proved the most practical, helped by strong security reductions and efficient arithmetic [1]. Its hardness comes from lattice problems such as Learning With Errors and its ring and module variants, for which no efficient algorithm is known on either classical or quantum hardware. After several rounds of review, NIST standardised the module-lattice signature scheme CRYSTALS-Dilithium as ML-DSA in FIPS 204 [3]. The original design and its security analysis are due to Ducas and colleagues [5], who showed that the scheme is strongly unforgeable while keeping keys and signatures to a workable size.

ML-DSA works in the ring R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^256 + 1) with degree n = 256 and prime modulus q = 8 380 417. Two operations dominate its cost. The first is polynomial multiplication, carried out through the NTT using a.b = INTT(NTT(a) (.) NTT(b)), where (.) is coefficient-wise multiplication. The second is the SHAKE extendable-output function, an instance of Keccak/SHA-3 [4], which ML-DSA uses as its only symmetric primitive: for matrix expansion (ExpandA), secret sampling (ExpandS), mask sampling (ExpandMask) and challenge hashing (H). Both operations are therefore the right targets for acceleration.

Hardware Implementations of the Keccak/SHAKE Primitive

Published Keccak hardware varies along two axes: how the 24 rounds are scheduled in time, and how the many independent SHAKE jobs are spread across hardware. On timing, the common choice in high-performance Dilithium work is one round per clock, with all five step mappings evaluated in a single cycle. Beckwith and colleagues [6], the compact TCHES design of Zhao and colleagues [10], and the open-source ML-DSA-OSH [9] all take this route. Unrolling two rounds per cycle, as Sundal and Chaves do on Virtex-7 [20], reaches very high throughput at a large area cost, while splitting a round across cycles trades bytes per cycle for a higher clock.

On the spatial side, fast designs almost always replicate the core and feed the copies from a dispatcher, exactly because ExpandA presents so many independent jobs [6], [9], [11]. A newer idea, LightHD [17], runs two cores 26 cycles out of phase so one absorbs while the other permutes, which hides permutation latency without doubling the area. Same-fabric comparisons for Cyclone V are scarce; the work of Tan and colleagues [21] on Cyclone V GX at about 140 MHz is the closest match and is the fairest single yardstick for the engine built here. Hardware Implementations of the NTT

Published NTT designs for Dilithium fall into two camps. The first is memory-based, or iterative: coefficients live in on-chip RAM and the butterflies read and write them each cycle through a conflict-free addressing scheme. The second is streaming, built from a fixed chain of butterflies and delay lines (a multi-path delay commutator) with no address logic, at the price of a long pipeline-fill delay. Within the memory-based camp the clear direction over 2024 to 2026 is the 2x2 butterfly tile, which handles two transform layers, four coefficients, per cycle. Both the Area-Time design [12] and ML-DSA-OSH [9] use it, and the latter rejects a 4x4 tile as too costly in area. EMINEM [11] takes a mixed-radix variant to the best published efficiency. Research Gap and Summary

Taken together, the literature points to a settled recipe: a memory-based NTT with a 2x2 conflict-free tile, and replicated one-round-per-cycle Keccak cores. Most of the headline numbers, though, come from faster Xilinx parts (Artix-7, Kintex and Zynq UltraScale+) and from AMD Versal, against which a Cyclone V part simply cannot win on raw clock speed. Two openings remain. First, there is very little Cyclone V Dilithium hardware in print that is verified against a golden model with full coverage; the rigour of verification is often left vague. Second, area-efficiency measures such as DSP count and area-time product are independent of the fabric and stay a fair contest. The thesis therefore aims for competitive cycle counts and strong resource efficiency on Cyclone V, every transaction checked and coverage closed, rather than a frequency record on a quicker device. The two finished modules and their results follow in Chapters 3 and 4.

Chapter 3

The Keccak / SHAKE Hashing Engine

Introduction

This chapter presents the first finished module of the accelerator, the Keccak/SHAKE hashing engine, built on the 1_hashing branch. It opens with the role of Keccak inside Dilithium, then sets out the design choices, the RTL, the UVM verification approach, and the results for verification, throughput and synthesis, before comparing the engine with published designs. The engine handles SHAKE128 and SHAKE256, closes functional coverage across four parallel lanes, and reaches 84.31 MHz on Cyclone V.

Role of Keccak in Dilithium

SHAKE is the only symmetric primitive in ML-DSA; there is no AES and no SHA-2 anywhere in the scheme. It serves both as a hash and as a deterministic source of randomness for sampling polynomials, as Table 3 sets out. By far the biggest consumer is ExpandA. At the top security level the matrix A is 8 by 7, so ExpandA runs 56 separate SHAKE128 instances, one per polynomial, each seeded by the public seed and the index pair. Because that index changes from call to call, the 56 jobs share no data and impose no ordering.

That independence is the key observation for the whole branch. SHAKE is not just a bottleneck; it is a bottleneck made of dozens of jobs that can run at once. The natural hardware response is therefore a replicated, multi-lane engine rather than a single faster core. Keccak itself rests on the sponge construction, drawn in Figure 3: a 1600-bit state divided into a rate r and a capacity c. The message is absorbed r bits at a time, each block XORed into the rate and followed by the Keccak-f[1600] permutation, after which output is squeezed r bits at a time. SHAKE128 uses a rate of 168 bytes and SHAKE256 a rate of 136 bytes.

Figure SEQ Figure \* ARABIC 3 The Keccak sponge construction, showing the absorb phase (XOR a message block into the rate, then permute) and the squeeze phase (read output, permute again when more is needed).

Design Decisions

Four choices shaped the RTL. All of them follow the same order of work: correctness first, then verified parallelism, and only then synthesis-driven tuning of the clock.

SHAKE only

The engine supports SHAKE128 and SHAKE256 and nothing else; the fixed-length SHA3-256 and SHA3-512 modes are left out. FIPS 204 uses SHAKE throughout and never calls the fixed modes, so adding them would only complicate the parameter and output logic for no gain. The two families differ only in a one-byte domain-separation suffix, while the permutation is identical.

A 64-bit data bus

The AXI4-Stream interface is 64 bits wide, or eight bytes per beat. The two SHAKE rates are 168 and 136 bytes, whose greatest common divisor is eight, so a 64-bit bus is the widest one that divides both rates cleanly and never has to straddle a rate boundary across two beats. A 256-bit bus would divide neither rate and would force carry-over logic into the absorb unit for only a modest gain, so it is left for later.

One round per cycle (baseline)

In the baseline the five step mappings are computed in a single cycle, giving 24 cycles per permutation. This keeps bytes per cycle as high as possible and matches the high-performance literature [6], [10]. The choice was measured before being revisited, and the synthesis story in Section 3.6 explains why that mattered.

Spatial parallelism with independent cores

N copies of the core are placed behind one parameterised wrapper, keccak_engine_parallel, with four lanes by default. This is the direct answer to ExpandA's many independent jobs: one core would process them in turn, whereas N lanes process N at once. The wrapper is the synthesisable top level of the branch, and a later controller will hand jobs out across the lanes.

RTL Architecture

Figure 4 shows the hierarchy. The wrapper instantiates N copies of keccak_core, and each core holds a parameter unit, an absorb unit, a step unit that contains the five step submodules, and an output unit. All parameters live in keccak_pkg.sv.

Each core runs a five-state machine, shown in Figure 5. IDLE latches the mode, rate, suffix and output length when a job starts. ABSORB XORs incoming bus beats into the state. SUFFIX_PADDING applies the SHAKE suffix and the pad byte at the rate boundary. PERMUTE runs the 24 rounds and then returns to ABSORB for a multi-block message or moves to SQUEEZE once absorption is finished. SQUEEZE reads output bytes, re-entering PERMUTE when more extendable output is needed, and returns to IDLE when the job ends.

Verification Methodology

The working testbench, tb_uvm_keccak_v2, is a UVM environment driving the four-lane wrapper. It uses four independent sets of agent, scoreboard and coverage collector, one per lane, with nothing shared between them, so any cross-lane corruption would show up at once as a mismatch. Several habits were adopted after an earlier testbench failed for want of them. Clocking blocks are avoided, since their one-cycle sampling skew had caused the monitor to miss handshakes; all sampling is done directly on the clock edge. The output-ready signal is driven by the monitor alone. A reset is asserted before every transaction so each test starts from a clean idle state. Driver and monitor hand off through a UVM event, so the driver never starts a new job before the monitor has finished the last one. Figure 7 shows the standard UVM layout and its phases.

Figure SEQ Figure \* ARABIC 7 Structure and phase schedule of the UVM environment, from test through environment to agent, scoreboard and coverage over the device under test.

Correctness is pinned to a golden model written entirely in SystemVerilog, keccak_ref_pkg.sv, which implements the full permutation, the sponge and both SHAKE modes with no C model or DPI. The stress and coverage sequences call this model during simulation to work out the expected output for each random message, so every transaction carries a precomputed reference and is genuinely compared; there is no path that skips checking. Each lane runs 52 tests, made up of 13 directed NIST vectors in both bounded and continuous output modes, 20 random tests and 19 coverage-closure tests, for 208 in total across the four lanes. Coverage crosses the mode against message length and output length.

Throughput and Parallelism

The parallelism here is about wall-clock cycles, not clock frequency. Chapter 4

The Number-Theoretic-Transform Engine

Introduction

This chapter covers the second finished module, the NTT engine, built on the 2_ntt branch. The NTT is how Dilithium multiplies polynomials, and it is the busiest arithmetic block in the accelerator. The engine computes the forward transform, the inverse transform with its 1/N scaling, and point-wise multiplication over the ring R_q = Z_q[x]/(x^256 + 1). It is feature-complete and fully verified, with the UVM regression at 53 of 53 and coverage closed, and it has been synthesised and tuned on Cyclone V to 75.44 MHz using only six DSP blocks. The chapter keeps the same shape as Chapter 3.

Role and Background

Multiplying two polynomials the schoolbook way costs order n squared operations. The NTT, a Fourier transform over a finite field, brings that down to order n log n through the identity a.b = INTT(NTT(a) (.) NTT(b)). Dilithium was chosen to suit the transform: because q is congruent to 1 modulo 2n, a 2n-th root of unity exists (zeta = 1753), and the negacyclic twist of x^256 + 1 folds straight into the twiddle factors. Since signing takes about three-quarters of the run time and is dominated by the polynomial multiplications in the rejection loop, the NTT is the focus of this branch.

In hardware the transform is built from butterflies, stage by stage, much like an FFT; for n = 256 there are eight radix-2 stages of 128 butterflies each. Two butterfly types are used, drawn in Figure 14. The Cooley-Tukey form (t = zeta.b; a' = a + t; b' = a - t) drives the forward transform, and the Gentleman-Sande form (a' = a + b; b' = zeta.(a - b)) drives the inverse. Cooley-Tukey takes natural order in and gives bit-reversed order out, while Gentleman-Sande does the reverse, so using one for the forward transform and the other for the inverse cancels the bit-reversal and saves a reordering pass.

Figure SEQ Figure \* ARABIC 14 The two butterfly datapaths: Cooley-Tukey for the forward transform and Gentleman-Sande for the inverse.

Design Decisions

A memory-based core with a 2x2 butterfly tile

The engine is memory-based and built around a 2x2 butterfly tile, which takes four coefficients in and out per cycle and collapses two radix-2 stages into one memory pass (Figure 15). Three reasons drove the choice. It is easy to verify, because the latency is fixed and deterministic and the golden model is simple. It follows current practice, since the strongest recent designs use the 2x2 tile [9], [12] and reject a 4x4 tile as too area-hungry. And it avoids the pipeline-fill penalty of a streaming design, which can waste around 250 cycles filling a delay line for a single transform. Inside the tile, the two first-rank results pass straight to the second-rank butterflies through multiplexers rather than going back to memory, which halves the memory traffic and is the main reason the tile earns its area. The eight radix-2 stages thus fold into four memory passes of 64 tiles each.

Figure SEQ Figure \* ARABIC 15 The 2x2 butterfly tile: four units in two ranks, with the dashed paths showing intra-tile forwarding from the first rank to the second, so no memory write happens between the two stages.

Modular reduction, from Barrett to a q-specific fold

Each butterfly contains one modular multiply, a.b mod q, and the way that reduction is done sets the critical path. The engine was first brought up with Barrett reduction, which works for any modulus and is easy to check, and only after the golden model and the UVM environment were clean was it changed to a q-specific shift-add fold. Because q equals 2^23 minus 2^13 plus 1, the relation 2^23 = 2^13 - 1 (mod q) lets the high part of a product collapse with shifts and additions alone: four folds bring the 46-bit product below twice the modulus, and one conditional subtraction lands it in range. Only the a.b multiply is left, since the reduction needs no multiplier (Figure 16). The change cut DSP use from 18 to 6 and raised the clock from 63 to 75 MHz, and because the latency stayed at three cycles, the butterfly and the core did not have to change.

Figure SEQ Figure \* ARABIC 16 The pipelined modular multiplier: one a.b multiply followed by four shift-add folds and a conditional subtraction, in place of Barrett's three multiplies.

Conflict-free four-bank memory

For the tile to read four coefficients and write four back every cycle without two accesses landing on the same RAM port, the flat 256 by 23 array is split into four banks of 64 by 23 with the XOR bank function bank(addr) = addr[1:0] xor addr[3:2] xor addr[5:4] xor addr[7:6]. Across all forward and inverse passes, every tile maps onto a permutation of the four banks, so each bank needs just one read and one write per cycle, which is exactly a Cyclone V dual-port M10K. A four-way crossbar routes the four tile positions to the four physical banks (Figure 17), and point-wise multiplication uses a second bank set for the other operand. The resolver approach was chosen over the alternative scheme because the latter's main benefit, single-port RAM, has no value on Cyclone V, whose block RAM is dual-port to begin with.

Figure SEQ Figure \* ARABIC 17 Conflict-free four-bank coefficient memory with read and write crossbars feeding the 2x2 tile; the XOR bank function guarantees one read and one write per bank per cycle.

Verification Methodology

The NTT testbench reuses the pattern proved out on the Keccak branch: no clocking blocks, a reset before each transaction, driver-to-monitor handoff through a UVM event, and every transaction compared against the golden model, with one agent driving the engine. The golden model, ntt_ref_pkg.sv, is a SystemVerilog port of the Dilithium reference forward and inverse transforms kept in the ordinary integer domain, together with point-wise multiplication and a separate negacyclic schoolbook multiply used as a cross-check. A standalone testbench self-verifies the model and passes 208 of 208 checks, covering the round trip INTT(NTT(a)) = a, the NTT-based product checked against the schoolbook, twiddle spot checks, edge cases and 100 random vectors. The UVM sequences cover directed edge cases, random stress over forward, inverse and round-trip operations, and coverage-closure crosses, with the expected results computed by the model as the sequence runs.

Chapter 5

Conclusions

Summary

This thesis set out to design and verify an efficient, modular hardware implementation of the CRYSTALS-Dilithium signature scheme, aimed at its two most demanding primitives. At the mid-year point both are complete, fully verified and synthesised on Intel Cyclone V. The Keccak/SHAKE engine handles SHAKE128 and SHAKE256, scales across four lanes at a measured 3.84 times, passes all 208 checked transactions with coverage closed, and reaches 84.31 MHz after a timing-guided optimisation pass. The NTT engine performs the forward and inverse transforms and point-wise multiplication with a 2x2 conflict-free tile and a q-specific reduction, passes all 53 transactions with coverage closed, and reaches 75.44 MHz on only six DSP blocks at a leading cycle count of about 299.

The objectives of analysing the reference model, writing synthesisable RTL for both primitives, verifying each against a trusted golden model with full coverage, and measuring area and frequency on FPGA have been met for the two finished modules. Two findings recur across both engines and are the most useful to carry forward. A golden model that compares every transaction is a practical way to earn confidence in correctness. And the path that looks like the bottleneck usually is not, so the post-fit timing report, rather than intuition, has to decide what gets pipelined. The benchmarking has been kept measured throughout: the engines do well on fabric-neutral metrics such as cycle count, DSP efficiency and verified scaling, while the clock gap to faster Xilinx and Versal parts is reported as a property of the device rather than hidden.

Recommendations for Future Work

Several directions remain for the second half of the thesis. The cheapest clock gain is to register the inputs of the modular multiplier, which would isolate the surviving multiply and is expected to take the NTT engine to somewhere near 110 to 120 MHz; the matching squeeze-side change would recover much of the Keccak gap to the same-fabric reference. A radix-4 butterfly and a decoupling input FIFO for the NTT engine are also worth weighing for further gains in area-time and in integration.

The main task ahead is integration: joining the two engines with a rejection sampler and a controller into a complete accelerator for key generation, signing and verification. The interface between blocks deserves the most care, because published work shows the heaviest cost in a full accelerator is the mismatch in data format between modules, so a pipelined path that reorders sampler output for the NTT is preferable to a plain store-and-reload buffer, and the number of Keccak lanes should be set by matching throughput to the measured sampler and NTT rates. Further out, side-channel protection such as masking, and a final characterisation in ASIC, would move the work towards deployment. Each of these builds on the verified, synthesised foundation that this report has put in place.
