As quantum computers become more affordable and commonplace, existing security systems that are based
on classical cryptographic primitives, such as RSA and Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC), will no longer
be secure. Hence, there has been interest in designing post-quantum cryptographic (PQC) schemes,
such as those based on lattice-based cryptography (LBC). The potential of LBC schemes is evidenced
by the number of such schemes passing the selection of NIST PQC Standardization Process Round-3. One
such scheme is the Crystals-Dilithium signature scheme, which is based on the hard module-lattice prob-
lem. However, there is no efficient implementation of the Crystals-Dilithium signature scheme. Hence, in
this article, we present a compact hardware architecture containing elaborate modular multiplication units
using the Karatsuba algorithm along with smart generators of address sequence and twiddle factors for
NTT, which can complete polynomial addition/multiplication with the parameter setting of Dilithium in
a short clock period. Also, we propose a fast software/hardware co-design implementation on Field Pro-
grammable Gate Array (FPGA) for the Dilithium scheme with a tradeoff between speed and resource uti-
lization. Our co-design implementation outperforms a pure C implementation on a Nios-II processor of the

1 INTRODUCTION
Quantum computation is fast becoming a reality and more commonplace. While there are benefits
associated with quantum computers (e.g., high-speed parallel processing and low energy consump-
tion), there are also implications to existing security systems that rely on classical cryptographic
primitives (e.g., RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC)). For example,
there are existing quantum algorithms that can efficiently solve the integer factorization problem
and discrete logarithm problem [43].
   Hence, there has been interest in post-quantum cryptography (PQC), whose security is based
on mathematical problems that are computationally hard to solve even using quantum algorithms.
The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has been assessing potential
post-quantum algorithms to be included in the next standard, where security level and implemen-
tation performance are two key evaluation criteria [3, 11]. Specifically, all submitted algorithms
will be subject to a public, competition-like process, and as of April 2020 a total of 15 round-three
candidates were selected, including seven finalists and eight alternate candidates.
   Of the seven round-three finalists, five are based on lattice-based cryptography (LBC). One
key advantage of LBC is that the problems they rely on (e.g., shortest vector problem (SVP), and
shortest independent vector problem (SIVP)) are computationally hard to solve under worst-
case hardness assumptions. In Ajtai’s [2] work, the small integer solution (SIS) problem is used
to build a family of potent one-way functions, where preimage computation means finding the
length of shortest lattice vector up to a polynomial factor. The learning with errors (LWE) has
also been proven to be computationally hard by a quantum reduction from SVP and SIVP [39],
and hence, not surprisingly, LWE has been utilized in many cryptography schemes [35] and the
relevant security reduction is tight.
   However, cryptographers may prefer the ring-version and module-version of SIS and LWE,
where the ideal lattice Zq /f (x ) is introduced. The preference is partly due to implementation
efficiency, as an ideal lattice implementation [28] has additional algebraic structure (compared to
the standard lattice). Hence, large-scale matrix multiplication can be replaced with polynomial
multiplication, which reduces the cost of arithmetical operations significantly. Consequently, the
corresponding lattice-based cryptosystems are more flexible and versatile.
   In lattice-based schemes involving ideal lattice Zq /f (x ), polynomial multiplications are a sig-
nificant proportion of arithmetic operations. Hence, there is renewed interest in fast polynomial
multiplication [8, 10, 21, 30]. The reduction polynomial often used in ideal lattice is f (x ) = x n + 1
because polynomial multiplication can be accelerated using the Number Theoretic Transform
(NTT) algorithm. NTT can be regarded as a variant of Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) [36],

and many FFT algorithms with different radixes have been proposed since the 1960s. However,
FFT based on radix-2,4,8 and split-radix are commonly used for implementation, especially the
algorithms of Cooley-Tukey [12] and Gentleman-Sande [19] because of their simplicity. The case
of NTT is no exception. The efficiency of NTT implementation largely depends on the designs of
dataflow, concurrency, throughput, and modular multiplication on Zq .
   Existing lattice-based schemes generally have a probabilistic inherent feature. Many of them
involve rejection sampling because the implementations of probability distribution are usually
highly correlated with a tradeoff between quality and efficiency. Also, it may be the most vulner-
able part when subject to side channel attacks (SCA). Uniform distribution is one of the easiest
to implement. However, as a true random number is costly to generate, researchers usually use
a pseudo random number generator to sample, like linear feedback shift register (LFSR) and
hash function. A common method of generating pseudo random sequences is to select a seed as the
input of an extendable-output hash function such as SHAKE128/SHAKE256 of the SHA-3 family,
and splicing the output continuously. The family of SHA-3 is the current hash function standard
selected by NIST.
   Crystals-Dilithium is a signature scheme constructed on the “Fiat-Shamir with aborts” frame-
work, and has been proven to be secure based on Module-LWE (MLWE) and Module-SIS
(MSIS) problems in the random oracle model. Also, Dilithium is one of the three digital signa-
tures finalists in round-three of the NIST PQC standardization Process. The public key size of
Dilithium is small, compared to many other LBC schemes. This is because it uses and improves
the technique of hints. The authors of Dilithium have also proposed a scheme (Dilithium-G),
where the involved error distribution is Gaussian distribution. However, in order to simplify the
sampling process, Dilithium is free of Gaussian distribution and only uniform distribution is used.
Compared to the most efficient signature scheme without Gaussian sampling, its public key space
cost is about 2.5 times less. The authors have implemented the Dilithium scheme on Intel Core-i7
4770k and an AVX2 optimization is also considered [15]. However, at the time of this research,
there is no efficient FPGA implementation reported of the Crystals-Dilithium scheme.
   In this article, we improve a classical hardware implementation for polynomial multiplication
using the NTT algorithm and present a compact hardware architecture for polynomial vector op-
erations, where both forward/inverse NTT and pointwise addition/multiplication with parameter
setting of Dilithium can be completed in a short clock period. In our architecture, finely designed
modular multiplication units and generators of both address access and twiddle factors are con-
tained, which help reduce latency and improve efficiency. Based on our architecture, we propose
FPGA implementations for the Crystals-Dilithium signature scheme on the platform Altera DE2-
115 and Xilinx ZYNQ-7020.
   A comparative summary between our implementation and existing classical implementa-
tions demonstrates that our architecture is suitable for embedded applications with limited
area. Our evaluation findings also show that our implementation is faster by 11.2 times for
signature and 7.4 times for verification, in comparison to the corresponding pure C imple-
mentation on a Nios-II processor of the platform DE2-115. As for ARM Cortex-A9 of ZYNQ-
7020, the rates of speed improvement are about 51% and 31% for signature and verification,
respectively.
   The structure of our article is organized as follows. In Sections 2 and 3, we will introduce
the related literature and relevant background knowledge. Section 4 presents our algorithms.
The hardware architecture based on our algorithms and the corresponding modules are given in
Sections 5 and 6, respectively. The performance evaluation and comparative summary are pre-
sented in Section 7. Finally, we conclude this article in the last section.

2 RELATED LITERATURE
Existing research on efficient implementation of LBC schemes based on ideal lattice mainly fo-
cuses on the components of polynomial multiplication with different algorithms like NTT and
Schoolbook. Existing implementations of polynomial multiplication can be broadly categorized
into those based on software and those based on hardware.
   In 2014, Roy et al. [40] implemented a compact hardware crypto-processor in which the NTT
algorithm was optimized in a way that inspired many subsequent architectures, including [38]
and [34] which extended the technique to inverse transformation. Seiler et al. [42] provided a fast
AVX2 optimized implementation of NTT by modifying the Montgomery reduction. Feng et al.
[17] designed a multi-lane stockham NTT architecture with high parallelism, which uses many
DSPs and can complete the transformations very quickly. Mert et al. [29] proposed a scalable
NTT architecture, where the modulus length varies from 22 to 32 bits and it performs well in the
Fan-Vercauteren (FV) homomorphic scheme. Schoolbook polynomial multiplication (SPM)
is another common method for compact LBC scheme implementation, due to its low resource cost.
Liu et al. [25] proposed an optimized implementation of SPM on Kintex-7.
   Hash function is also widely studied, and the current international standard SHA-3 is de-
scribed by NIST. Implementation of SHA-3 has a number of advantages because of its slice-
oriented architecture [16]. Considering the optimization approaches of unrolling and pipelining,
Wong et al. [45] presented an architecture with an optimal tradeoff between speed and resource
cost. In the work of [32], a novel SHA-3 implementation involving the technology of ReRAM-
based in memory-computing (IMC) was proposed, designed to achieve significant energy
reduction.
   In [33], the authors surveyed existing implementations of the entire lattice-based cryptosys-
tems. At present, there is an efficient open source C++ library, FNLlib [1], which is continuously
improved and can be used for polynomial arithmetic operations in LBC schemes based on ideal
lattice. However, the corresponding hardware implementations are limited. The first hardware im-
plementation of the PKE scheme based on RLWE was proposed by Göttert et al. [20] in 2012. In
2014, an implementation of the BLISS signature scheme on Xilinx Spartan-6 FPGA was introduced
[37], where the CDT sampler was improved. For standard lattices, Howe et al. [22] presented an en-
cryption engine based on the standard LWE problem, which incurs significantly less area resource
on the lightweight Spartan-6 platform. Kuo et al. [24] proposed a hardware implementation of the
NewHope scheme, which they claimed to be the first.
   Recently, Basu et al. [7] presented a hardware-based comparison of many popular PQC schemes,
including the Crystals-Dilithium (the focus in our work). However, the designs they discussed were
generated by High-Level Synthesis (HLS) methods, which can be seen as heuristic but not prac-
tical hardware designs on many platforms. Banerjee et al. [6] provided a configurable processor for
many LBC protocols, which can implement sampling, modular arithmetic, and polynomial multi-
plication efficiently. In the work of Alkim et al. [4], an optimized RISC-V implementation of the
polynomial arithmetic used by the Kyber and NewHope scheme is proposed, designed to achieve
better performance by extending optimizations for the ARM instruction set to RISC-V. Dang et al.
[13] provided software/hardware co-design for three lattice-based key encapsulation mechanisms
and demonstrated that such an approach outperforms purely software implementation. Fritzmann
et al. [18] presented an intensive RISC-V architecture embedding a number of powerful accelera-
tors to speed up LBC schemes, whose performance has been evaluated by ASIC implementation.
In addition, Xin et al. [46] provided a domain-specific vector processor that supports parallel num-
ber theoretical transforms and vector sampling. Their architecture outperforms the performance
in the approach [6] by an order of magnitude.

3   PRELIMINARIES
In this section, we provide an overview of MLWE and MSIS problems, polynomial multiplication
on ring Zq /f (x ), random sampling, and the Crystals-Dilithium scheme.

3.1 MlWE and MSIS
According to Regev [39], LWE is conjectured to be hard to solve, assuming the hardness of both
SVP and SIVP. Thus, LWE can be used to create public-key cryptosystems, which are more secure
than classical ones. A matrix A ∈ Zqk×n with large dimensions, whose elements sampled from an
identical distribution and independent of each other, results in significant savings for calculation.
So rather than standard lattices, many lattice-based cryptosystems are based on ideal lattice, which
enjoys additional algebra structure introduced in Lyubashevsky et al.’s work [26–28].
   It is widely believed that the special structure of ideal lattices does not supply a shortcut [41].
However, in order to strike a balance between implementation efficiency and security level, MLWE
was introduced in [9], which can be seen as a generalization of both LWE and RLWE.
   The MLWE problem is defined as follows: Suppose A(x ) comes from Rqk×l randomly and uni-
formly, S (x ) comes from Rql , and E (x ) comes from Rqk according to a specific k-dimension (l-
dimension) distribution, where k and l are usually much less than the degree n. The target is to
find secret polynomial vector S (x ) given A(x ) and B(x ), where B(x ) = A(x )S (x ) + E (x ). l is called
the module rank. This is the RLWE problem, when l is set as one. Figure 1 presents the matrix
description of MLWE.
   The MSIS problem is defined as follows: Given A ∈ Rqk×l chosen from a specific distribution, the
target is to find z ∈ Rql , which satisfies Az = 0 and 0 < ||z||∞ ≤ β. Here, β is a bound parameter.
The norm restriction is necessary, otherwise the problem will be much easier by the Gaussian
elimination method. Actually, in some sense SIS and LWE are dual problems to each other because
b = As + e is equivalent to Bt = 0, where B = (A|I ) and t = (s T , e T − bT )T . This transformable re-
lationship holds analogously in the case of the module-version.

3.2 Polynomial Multiplication on Zq /f (x )
Practical fast polynomial multiplication over Rq has always been a topic of interest. The multipli-
cation in the form of coefficients is computationally intensive because of the convolution calcula-
tion. In comparison, the counterpart in the form of function value needs just n pointwise multi-
plications, which is much easier. The transformation from expression of coefficients to values also
implies quadratic computation complexity in general. However, selecting special points and poly-
nomial f (x ), we can simplify the transformation computation complexity from O (n2 ) to O (nloдn).
This results in overall improvement for speed. The fast transformation algorithm is NTT.
   Choosing f (x ) = x n − 1 and 1, w n1 , . . . , w nn−1 to be the n value points (here, w k is the k-th prim-
itive root on Zq ), the correctness of NTT can be deduced from the fact that c (x ) = a(x )b (x ) ∈
Zq /x n − 1 is equivalent to c (w ni ) = a(w ni )b (w ni ), (i = 0, 1, . . . , n − 1). The efficiency improvement
is based on properties of primitive root w k . An in-depth discussion of NTT is presented in [12, 19].

3.3 Sampling and Hash
Random sampling is one of the indispensable components of public-key cryptosystems. The secu-
rity of cryptosystems depends heavily on the quality of random numbers, or specifically, the ran-
domness and independence. The acquisition of true random source is costly, so in practice pseudo-
random number generator (PRNG) is used. PRNG can efficiently generate long pseudo-random
sequences resistant to statistical analysis.
   A common PRNG method is to select a short random seed and then use a hash function to
extend it. In the Dilithium scheme, the extended random bytes come from the states of Keccak.
As the random numbers of many cryptosystems must be from particular ranges, the rejection
sampling is usually involved. All the hash functions used in the Dilithium scheme are from the
SHA-3 after some necessary modification.

3.4 Crystals-Dilithium Signature Scheme
The Crystals-Dilithium is one of the three round-three finalists signature algorithms in the NIST
PQC Standardization Process. The security of Dilithium is based on the hardness of MLWE and
MSIS, and the security level varies due to parameters k and l, while Rq is constant. This property
eases implementation. The modulus q = 8,380,417 and degree of polynomial n = 256 do not change
with the security level. We refer readers interested in other detailed descriptions and analysis to
the supporting documentation submitted to NIST [15].

4.1 Modular Multiplication on Zq

scheme. In our hardware design, we use the Karatsuba algorithm [23] for the standard multipli-

section. For Montgomery reduction, we choose r = 223 , and the corresponding q  = 223 − 213 − 1

5.1    Hardware Modules
The core components of our hardware architecture are responsible for operations on polyno-
mial vectors, or specifically, the forward/inverse-NTT and pointwise polynomial multiplication/
addition. Moreover, we also consider involving Keccak into our hardware architecture. This is
because we experimentally found that the proportion of implementation cost for hash function
increases rapidly and becomes a new bottleneck after the polynomial operations are implemented
on the hardware. Our hardware implementation for Keccak is somewhat straightforward, and
hence we do not provide further details. On the platform ZYNQ-7020, we experimentally found
that, as the software part is implemented on an ARM processor with high performance and has
an independent clock drive with a high frequency, introducing Keccak into hardware exclusively
does not result in considerable improvements. To achieve increased improvement, we optimize
Keccak using the NEON vector instructions of Cortex-A9. The results of our profiling experiments
and comparison with several existing works are presented in the following sections.

for every cycle may be uneven. However, it does not matter because the bottleneck of critical path
and frequency lies on other modules instead of this part.

5.3   Scheduling of Twiddle Factors
Both butterfly operation and the on-the-fly generation of twiddle factors involve modular mul-
tiplication. To ensure that the butterfly operation is performed once per clock cycle, we need to
assign several registers for the twiddle factors to work and renew in rotation to deal with the cycle
delays of the pipeline. In Roy’s work [40], a look-up table is used to select the corresponding w of
the current cycle, while we use the method of register rotation to replace it so that many hardware
selectors can be removed and the critical path is shortened. This method works because the clocks
of every NTT stage are constant (128 clocks in Dilithium). Also, the buffer of Dout-low in [40] is
not needed.
   However, the additional cost is that we must store the initial values of all twiddle factor se-
quences. The used w’s of different NTT stages are completely different from each other. Actually,
there is km = 2m l for all the factors used in the m-th NTT stage, where l is odd and km is the
exponent of base w 2n . Therefore, we need to store 15 + 7 + 3 + 1 = 26 factors more for forward-
NTT and 14 + 6 + 2 = 22 factors more for inverse-NTT, which is a considerable cost. In [40], a
technique of parallelization and factors reuse is involved so the factors necessary to store are less.

5.4 Generation of Address Sequence
The polynominals expected to execute the NTT algorithm are stored in the dual-port RAM.
The whole algorithm can be regarded as repeatedly reading the data from the specific address,
processing it using the arithmetic module, and writing it back. We use the access scheme in [40]
but the delay between read signal and write signal needs to be modified according to the pipeline
constraint in our own architecture. A core idea of this algorithm is continuously processing
the input data pairs of butterfly operation with the same twiddle factors in every NTT stage while
jumping with the generation order of twiddle factors. Fortunately, the transformation law can be
easily implemented by a hardware counter with trivial bits adjustment, which has been presented
in the next section and Figure 3(b). In addition, for both forward/inverse NTT and pointwise
multiplication/addition, the done signal is produced by the address module because it can directly
and conveniently reflect whether the corresponding algorithms have been finished.

7.3     FPGA Implementation of the Scheme
For the scheme implementation, we use a common FPGA design methodology of starting with a
pure software implementation on the embedded processor, offloading the most time-consuming
tasks to hardware in proper order and acquiring the design with a satisfactory tradeoff between
area and speed. The polynomial operations, especially multiplication, is a hot topic for researchers
on lattice-based cryptography, because it takes up a large part of computation in many LBC
schemes, including Dilithium. In our implementation of signature, we use our polynomial op-
eration components to achieve more than a half saving of time on our Cyclone platform and 39%
on the Zynq platform. As for verification, also about a half of time is saved on Cyclone and the
percentage saving is 18% for Zynq. The profiling results are included in Tables 4 and 5. Our sec-
ond concern is the hash function, or Keccak to be exact, involved in Dilithium because it became
the new bottleneck after we offloaded the polynomial multiplication/addition into hardware. For
Cyclone, we get a second saving of about 70–80% for both signature and verification. But the im-
provements on the Zynq platform may be not be substantial, which is only around 3% considering
the addition cost of area, so we try using NEON vector instructions to replace the original C code
and acquire a more satisfactory improvement. After our measurement, we found that there is no
considerable operation that is too time-consuming among the remaining software parts, so we do
not design additional optimization techniques.
   From the experiment results, in Cyclone, compared to the pure soft-core implementation on
Nios II, the design including both polynomial operation and Keccak is approximately 11.2 times
faster for signature and 7.4 times faster for verification. While on the Xilinx Zynq-7020, the built-in
Cortex A9 processor, which has a great performance on its own and is driven by a high-frequency
clock independent of the hardware modules, significantly improves the implementation speed of
the C part. The improvement brought by offloading Keccak into hardware seems not very signifi-
cant, so in the final design selection on Zynq we use the NEON version. Our polynominal hardware
architecture still helped achieve 39% and 18% speed improvement for signature and verification,
respectively, compared to the pure C implementation on Cortex A9. Lastly, the final improvements
are 51.1% and 30.9% for signature and verification, respectively. In addition, we also provide two

8 CONCLUSION
Polynomial multiplication and SHA-3 Keccak function are two of the most computationally inten-
sive operations in the Crystals-Dilithium scheme. In this article, we proposed an improved NTT
architecture, by building on the work of Roy et al. [40]. We also presented cooperative implementa-
tions of the Dilithium signature scheme in both Cyclone and Zynq environment at a low resource
cost. We demonstrated that on our Altera DE2-115 platform, our implementation is about 11.2
times faster for signature and 7.4 times faster for verification than the corresponding pure Nios II
software implementation. In addition, we also achieved 51% and 31% speed improvement for sig-
nature and verification compared to the pure C implementation on ZYNQ-7020 platform with a
high-performance ARM processor. In the future, we intend to explore alternative optimized im-
plementation architectures to achieve better performance.
