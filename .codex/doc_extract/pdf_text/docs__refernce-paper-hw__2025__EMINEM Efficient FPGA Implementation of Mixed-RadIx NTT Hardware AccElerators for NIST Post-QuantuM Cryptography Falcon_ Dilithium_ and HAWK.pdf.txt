EMINEM: Efficient FPGA Implementation of Mixed-RadIx
NTT Hardware AccElerators for NIST Post-QuantuM
Cryptography Falcon, Dilithium, and HAWK

YAZHENG TU and JIAFENG XIE, Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering,
Villanova University, Villanova, Pennsylvania, USA


The advent of quantum computing poses a significant threat to modern cryptography. To address this chal-
lenge, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has initiated the Post-Quantum
Cryptography (PQC) standardization process, and several algorithms have been selected (a few are still
under consideration in the additional standardization process). Among these schemes, lattice-based PQC has
emerged as a promising approach and garnered substantial attention from the implementation community,
especially on the hardware platforms. Notably, the Field-Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) has gained
considerable attention as a convenient platform for hardware implementation, not only from NIST but also
from the research community, as reflected by the related recommendations from NIST and the number of
works reported recently.
   This work follows the existing trend of developing novel FPGA implementations for PQC. It is worth
mentioning that the polynomial multiplication in these NIST lattice-based PQC algorithms can be implemented
with Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) for efficiency. Nevertheless, there remains a lack of novel and
universal NTT methods for polynomial multiplication at different sizes. For instance, for 𝑛 = 512 (Falcon and
HAWK), the existing works are mostly limited to the Radix-2 NTT (other methods like Radix-4 or Radix-8
cannot be directly applied). To fill the research gap, this article presents a novel design framework, i.e.,
Efficient Mixed-RadIx NTT hardware accElerators for NIST post-quantuM cryptography (EMINEM),
specially tailored for targeted schemes. Our design leverages Radix-4 for polynomial sizes of 256 and 1,024,
while introducing a hybrid Radix-2/4 strategy for NTT of length 512 and achieving comparable performance
to pure Radix-4 at other lengths. In total, our contributions include: (i) a generic Radix-4/Mixed-Radix NTT
algorithm is proposed for 𝑛 = 256, 512, and 1,024; (ii) an efficient NTT hardware accelerator is designed
with the help of a new memory access pattern and some optimization techniques; (iii) two types of butterfly
architectures are developed to obtain pure Radix-4 time complexity and low resource usage, respectively;
(iv) a detailed implementation and comparison showcase the superior performance of the proposed design
strategy. Overall, the proposed strategy enables the efficient deployment of Mixed-Radix NTT in targeted
NIST schemes, surpassing the limitations of the conventional Radix-2 approach for 512-length NTT designs.
The proposed design offers a significant advancement in the field, facilitating efficient FPGA acceleration of
PQC standards.

1   Introduction
Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) has gained substantial attention from the research commu-
nity recently, as the existing public-key cryptosystems are proven to be insecure against quantum
attacks [4, 25, 29]. Among the proposed PQC schemes, lattice-based cryptography constitutes an
important PQC category due to its strong security proof and relative ease of implementation. In
particular, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) PQC standardization
process has selected the five algorithms for standardization, and three of them are lattice-based
schemes (including Kyber, Dilithium, and Fast Fourier Lattice-based Compact Signatures over
NTRU (Falcon)) [24]. Meanwhile, the additional NIST PQC digital signature “competition” pro-
cess also involves a lattice-based scheme HAWK [13]. In any case, these mentioned PQC algorithms
have gained significant attention recently from the implementation community, specifically on the
hardware platforms [29].
   Following this trend, a number of hardware implementation works have been carried out for
the efficient acceleration of PQC schemes and/or primitives (major components) on the Field-
Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) platforms, e.g., [3, 19, 31]. Overall, FPGAs are crucial for
accelerating PQC algorithms due to their unique architectural advantages. Firstly, in contrast to
CPUs, which execute instructions serially, FPGAs support true hardware parallelism, allowing
extensive bit- or word-level parallelization and enabling different cryptographic operations or
stages to be processed simultaneously. Secondly, the reconfigurability of FPGAs is a key feature that
provides significant benefits compared to CPUs and Application-Specific Integrated Circuits
(ASICs). Unlike ASICs, which are a one-time, fixed-function design, FPGAs can be precisely
reprogrammed to match the specific computational requirements of an algorithm. This allows for
customized optimizations for various PQC operations and parameters. It also offers the flexibility
for researchers to span a broad range of design categories, from highly optimized, low-complexity
solutions for resource-constrained environments to high-parallelism, high-speed designs for high-
performance scenarios, making FPGA an exceptionally adaptable platform for diverse design
requirements. Additionally, FPGAs offer excellent system-level integration due to their customizable
I/O structures, which enable seamless communication with other devices, such as CPUs and external
memory. This makes them a very adaptable platform for a variety of PQC hardware designs, from
resource-constrained to high-performance applications. In fact, NIST also recommends the Artix-7
FPGA as the hardware implementation platform for PQC algorithms [20].
   In terms of the actual implementation target on the FPGA platform, among the mentioned
lattice-based PQC schemes, polynomial multiplication over the ring can be regarded as one of
the most important components. Consequently, Number Theoretic Transform (NTT), a type
of Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) tailored for finite fields [21], has been widely deployed in
various mentioned lattice-based PQC schemes. NTT, in general, allows for rapid computation
of polynomial multiplication at the complexity of O (𝑛log𝑛), and hence is essential for different
operational phases of these PQC algorithms. Note that in this article, we target the general NTT

used in the mentioned NIST PQC schemes Falcon, HAWK, and Dilithium (Kyber uses a special
type of NTT [14], and hence is out of the scope of this work). Both Falcon and Dilithium have
been standardized by NIST as digital signature algorithms, making a unified hardware accelerator
crucial for practical deployment. These algorithms, along with candidates like HAWK (which is in
the second round of the additional digital signature schemes), require different parameters and
radices. For example, Falcon utilizes 𝑛 values of 512 and 1,024, while Dilithium employs an 𝑛
value of 256. Our design provides a solution for this multi-scheme environment by supporting
different n values and radices. This flexibility allows a single hardware platform to efficiently
execute multiple NIST-selected PQC schemes, providing the ability to adapt to different security
applications and standards without the need for reconfiguration. Specifically, as there are only a
few works released for Falcon and HAWK (no hardware implementation work has been released
for HAWK), we hope this work can stimulate many follow-up explorations, focusing on the
efficient hardware acceleration of NTT and even whole schemes for these PQC algorithms on
FPGA platforms.

1.1   Prior Works
Several hardware implementations (related to FPGAs) of NTT accelerators have been released for
the targeted lattice-based PQC schemes. While the specific NTT designs for Falcon are still not
abundant, we do find some NTT architectures using the same parameter settings of Falcon. A
high-throughput and energy-efficient pipelined NTT was proposed in [31], but requires bit-reversed
ordered input polynomials which increases the logic complexity for control unit. A low-complexity
NTT architecture was presented in [32], and an efficient NTT design was introduced in [30].
However, the proposed designs are based exclusively on Radix-2, and the proportional increase in
delay with each additional butterfly limits the achievable speedup. Authors of [19] proposed an
optimized hardware accelerator for NTT but relies on a high usage of DSPs, while authors of [33]
propose an NTT design with a low-complexity memory mapping scheme with a Fixed-Radix-4
selection which limits the range of polynomial length 𝑛. A scalable radix-2/4 NTT multiplication
architecture with an efficient conflict-free memory mapping scheme was proposed in [5], and a
hardware/software co-design of the NTT accelerator was introduced in [9]. The proposed designs
are limited to a single radix, which either introduces length range limitations [9] or increases
resource usage when scaling performance by adding more butterfly units [5].
   Similarly, a number of NTT works on the FPGA platform for Dilithium have been proposed
recently. Li et al. [16] presented a scalable and parallel-optimized NTT design on the FPGA plat-
form. The DSP and BRAM usage increases linearly with the number of butterflies, which leads
to a redundant and inefficient use of resources. Land et al. [15] introduced an optimized NTT
component making extensive use of DSPs on modern low-end FPGAs. Zhou et al. [34] proposed
an efficient NTT design for software/hardware co-designed Dilithium. Both of the proposed de-
signs require a relatively high number of DSPs, yet they do not achieve a commensurate im-
provement in speed. Derya et al. [6] proposed a configurable hardware architecture of NTT for
polynomial multiplier used in lattice-based cryptography. However, the resource consumption is
prohibitively large, rendering the design impractical for real-world applications. Wang et al. [27]
presented a Radix-4 NTT architecture with a conflict-free memory mapping scheme for Dilithium,
which involves high LUT usage, and thus is not suitable for resource-constraint designs. Very
recently, Mandal and Roy [18] have reported a standalone, fully pipelined, and scalable Radix-2
Dilithium NTT unit with reduced area and enhanced frequency but did not explore different
radix options.
   Note that, to the authors’ best knowledge, no NTT hardware design has been released based on
HAWK’s parameter setting.

1.2     Challenge and Motivation
Overall, existing design structures for NTT (of the targeted PQC) have three significant limitations
that hinder their flexibility and applicability. Firstly, they rely on a fixed-radix that is only suitable
for a specific value of the parameter 𝑛. This means that most existing works are limited to using
Radix-2 or Radix-4 for specific values of 𝑛, such as 𝑛 = 256 or 𝑛 = 1,024, but are restricted to
only using Radix-2 for 𝑛 = 512. This is because when 𝑛 = 512, the length of the input polynomial
is a power-of-2, but not a power-of-4, making Radix-4 inapplicable in this case. This limitation
restricts the design’s adaptability and efficiency, as it cannot be easily modified to accommodate
different values of 𝑛. Furthermore, existing designs for the second case mentioned above, i.e.,
not a power-of-4 (𝑛 = 512), rely solely on increasing the number of butterflies, which leads to
a linear increase in resource costs as the number of butterflies increases. While this approach
reduces latency, it results in a suboptimal tradeoff between time and resources, particularly in
terms of Area and Delay Product (ADP) efficiency. This means that the increased resources
required to reduce latency come at a disproportionate cost, making the solution less efficient
overall. Lastly, the existing works’ modular reduction and multiplication can be made more efficient
by optimizing their frequency and resource usage, potentially achieving improvements in the
overall NTT. Therefore, adopting a flexible Radix-2/4 design is more practical than using dedicated
accelerators for each PQC instance. While dedicated designs are optimized for single parameters,
they require time-consuming reconfiguration to switch between security levels, which is not feasible
for real-world applications. A Mixed-Radix design, in contrast, creates a unified accelerator that
supports a range of 𝑛 values without any reconfiguration penalty, allowing for seamless transitions
via control signals. Additionally, this approach is more area-efficient. Instead of creating multiple,
large hardware blocks, our design reuses a single, reconfigurable core. This shared resource usage
significantly reduces the hardware footprint, making the design more scalable and flexible for
different PQC deployments.

1.3     Major Contributions
With the above considerations, a Mixed-Radix NTT with efficiency seems to be necessary, as it
offers a more versatile solution that can maintain a consistent speed while adapting to different
values of 𝑛 with low resource cost. Following this direction, this article proposes a Mixed-Radix
NTT implementation for the targeted NIST PQC on FPGA platforms. Specifically, we propose a
novel generic design framework, i.e., Efficient Mixed-radIx NTT hardware accElerators for
NIST post-quantuM cryptography (EMINEM). The theoretical time complexity of the proposed
design equals log4𝑛 for 𝑛 = 256, 512, and 1,024, though Radix-4 NTT cannot be directly used
for 𝑛 = 512. To the authors’ best knowledge, this is the first swift Mixed-Radix NTT hardware
implementation for the mentioned NIST PQC schemes (that can switch between pure Radix-4 mode
and Mixed-Radix-2/4 mode). The main contributions of the article include:
      — We have rigorously formulated the major arithmetic operation of the NTT into desired Mixed-
        Radix forms to lay the mathematical foundation for the proposed hardware accelerator design
        strategy on the FPGA platform.
      — We employed the derived Mixed-Radix strategy to develop a versatile Radix-4/Mixed-Radix
        NTT accelerator that efficiently handles different polynomial sizes.
      — We introduced two distinct Mixed-Radix NTT (butterfly) architectures: a high-performance
        variant with uniform log4 𝑛 latency for both 𝑛 = 512 and 𝑛 = 1,024, and a compact, resource-
        efficient design with a slightly minor tradeoff in speed.
      — We implemented the accelerators for the targeted different NIST PQC schemes on various
        FPGA devices and performed optimization according to the features of each scheme.

2     Preliminaries
2.1     Targeted NIST PQC
In July 2022, the NIST PQC standardization process finally selected four algorithms, including the
targeted Falcon and Dilithium [24]. Besides that, the additional round of PQC digital signature
competition was also launched [22], and many algorithms have been submitted, e.g., the lattice-
based scheme HAWK [13] (which was promoted to the second round recently [23]). Note that in
alignment with HAWK, we still use the original names of Falcon and Dilithium here, though NIST
standards Dilithium as Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA) [8].
Brief introductions to these PQC algorithms are described below:
      — Falcon. Falcon is a CCA2 (Indistinguishability under Adaptive Chosen Ciphertext Attack)
        digital signature scheme standardized by NIST recently [24]. Overall, Falcon signature scheme
        can be described as: Falcon = GPV framework + NTRU lattices + Fast Fourier sampling [2],
        to provide NIST Level I/V security depending on the parameter selection. The parameters
        settings are 𝑛 = 512, 𝑞 = 12,289 and 𝑛 = 1,024, 𝑞 = 12,289 for Falcon-512 and Falcon-1,024,
        respectively. Among all NIST-selected algorithms, Falcon is known for its compact key size
        and fastest signature verification [2]. The detailed algorithm of Falcon can be seen at [2].
      — Dilithium. Dilithium is a digital signature scheme that is strongly secure under chosen message
        attacks [1]. The security is based on the hardness of lattice problems over module-lattices
        [1]. It has the smallest public-key + signature size of any lattice-based signature scheme that
        only uses uniform sampling [1]. Besides, it has three security levels—Dilithium2, Dilithium3,
        Dilithium5, and all three levels share 𝑛 = 256 and 𝑞 = 8,380,417 [1]. The details of Dilithium
        can be seen at [1] (ML-DSA can be seen at [8]).
      — HAWK . HAWK is a signature scheme inspired by the lattice Isomorphism problem, providing
        NIST Level I/V security [13]. It is based on the hardness of the one more (approximate)
        Shortest Vector Problem (omSVP) and the search module-Lattice Isomorphism Prob-
        lem (smLIP) [13]. There are two security levels for HAWK, namely HAWK-512 and HAWK-
        1,024, where 𝑛 = 512 and 𝑛 = 1,024, respectively. The authors did not specify the prime 𝑞 but
        used 𝑞 = 18,433 as a demonstration in the specification (software implementation) [13]. The
        detailed algorithm of HAWK can be seen at [13].
  Note here we do not implement our design on Kyber since normal NTT is not suitable to be
applied to Kyber directly due to the absence of the 2𝑛-primitive root of unity of 𝑞 = 3,329 when
𝑛 = 256. The NTT itself is valid in this case since the 𝑛th primitive of 𝑞 = 3,329 still exists, however,
we need to multiply the 2𝑛-primitive root with the coefficients in order to complete the polynomial
multiplication (this absence makes the normal NTT not applicable).

2.2     NTT
The NTT is a fast algorithm for multiplying large polynomials over finite fields [21], derived from
the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm. NTT offers a significant advantage in computation reduction

(compared with the schoolbook complexity of O (𝑛 2 )), making it a crucial component in various
lattice-based PQC schemes such as those mentioned above.
   NTTs can be implemented using different radices, including Radix-2 and Radix-4, which have a
significant impact on performance.
   Radix-2 NTT is the most common implementation method, using a binary tree structure to
compute the NTT in O (𝑛 · log2𝑛) time. The provided Algorithm 1 outlines the procedure for a
Radix-2 Gentleman–Sande NTT [10]. This algorithm’s structure is based on a Decimation-In-
Frequency (DIF) approach, as evident from the outer loop that iterates downwards from 𝑠 = 𝑚 − 1
to 0. The loop controls the stages of the algorithm, processing the data with a dynamically changing
stride. The nested inner loops then systematically execute the core “butterfly” computations. The
butterfly operation is a two-part process that calculates both the sum and the difference of two
inputs, 𝑢 and 𝑣. A critical aspect of this operation is the modular multiplication of the second input,
𝑣, by a pre-computed twiddle factor (𝑊 𝑗 mod 𝑞). These factors, derived from a primitive root of
unity, are essential for ensuring the correct transformation across all stages of the algorithm.
   Radix-4 NTT, on the other hand, uses a quaternary tree structure, reducing the number of modular
multiplications and achieving better performance in certain scenarios, with a time complexity of
O (𝑛 · log4𝑛). The provided Algorithm 2 outlines a Radix-4 Gentleman–Sande NTT [10], which,
similar to its Radix-2 counterpart, employs a DIF approach. The core structure involves a descending
outer loop that controls the stages of computation. Unlike the Radix-2 algorithm, which processes
data in pairs, the Radix-4 algorithm operates on groups of four elements at a time within its butterfly
operation. This four-point butterfly is significantly more complex, involving the combination
of four inputs (𝑢 0, 𝑢 1, 𝑢 2, and 𝑢 3 ) and the application of multiple twiddle factors. The twiddle
factors (𝑊 𝑙 , 𝑤 2𝑙 , 𝑤 3𝑙 , etc.) are powers of 𝜔 4 and are applied to each of the three non-first inputs

multiplications compared to a Radix-2 algorithm for the same polynomial size, which can lead

2.3    FPGA Acceleration for PQC
As mentioned above, FPGAs are essential for accelerating PQC (including schemes and important
components) due to their parallel processing capabilities and hardware flexibility, and have been
widely adopted in both academic and industry studies. NIST’s recommendation of the Xilinx Artix-7
FPGA [20] as the standard evaluation platform for PQC implementations further endorses the
advantages, demonstrating its effectiveness in handling complex PQC operations, such as the NTT.
By following NIST’s recommendation, our implementation maintains alignment with established
benchmarks, enabling meaningful performance comparisons and contributing to the advancement
of efficient PQC implementations.

values, each multiplied by a specific twiddle factor, before being stored in their final positions. This
dual-purpose structure allows the algorithm to be both flexible and highly parallelizable, making it
suitable for hardware implementations that need to support different polynomial sizes without
requiring entirely separate architectures.

4     EMINEM: Accelerator Details
Brief Introduction. Building upon the foundational algorithmic work, the practical implementation
of our proposed Mixed-Radix NTT (EMINEM) is designed to fully leverage the strengths of FPGAs.
As previously discussed, FPGAs offer the crucial parallelism and reconfigurability necessary to
translate complex mathematical operations into efficient hardware. The components of EMINEM
are specifically designed to leverage these FPGA features, thereby maximizing performance and
efficiency. By mapping these units onto FPGA elements, such as LUT, DSP, and Memory, the
proposed EMINEM achieves high calculation efficiency while maintaining the flexibility to adapt
to different 𝑛 values and optimizing resource usage.

4.1    Architectural Details
Following the proposed algorithm, in this section, we introduce the architectural details of the
proposed accelerator (EMINEM). As shown in Figure 2, the proposed EMINEM includes four major
components: Butterfly Unit, Control Unit, Output Sequencer, and Data Storage. When the proposed
accelerator starts to work, the control unit first decides if the Radix-2 NTT should be executed and
calculates the corresponding addresses and the selection signals (for all the RAM banks that are
storing the coefficients as well as the ROM where the twiddle factors are stored), and finally sends
them to the butterfly unit (if Radix-2 is not needed in the first stage, then the accelerator directly
executes Radix-4 format). The butterfly unit fetches the corresponding coefficients according to
the addresses received to calculate and delivers the transformed results to the output sequencer
(where the results are ordered and sent back to the RAM banks). The same process lasts until
the entire NTT is completed, and the final results (where the transformed coefficients are in the
bit/digit-reversed order) are stored in the RAM banks.
   Control Unit. As stated above, the control unit is responsible for generating all control signals,
including the selection signals, reading/writing address for each RAM bank, and the Radix-2 NTT
indication signal. Details of the address calculation, RAM bank selection, as well as radix switching,
are given in the following part of this subsection.
   The memory mapping scheme is the most critical component of the NTT hardware design.

any pipelined registers. This ensures all outputs are written back to the RAM banks accordingly to
accelerate the Radix-2 stage processing.

4.2     Brief Summary
The proposed architectures, with the pure Radix-4 butterfly unit and the resource-friendly butterfly
unit, execute NTT on power-of-4 point polynomials with no time difference since the time com-
plexity for both architectures is the same (𝑂 (𝑛 · 𝑙𝑜𝑔4 (𝑛))) with a pipelined delay (here is 7 clock
cycles). However, when 𝑛 is not a power-of-4, the architecture with pure Radix-4 butterfly unit
takes 𝑂 (𝑛/4 + 2 · 𝑙𝑜𝑔4 (𝑛/2)) cycles while the one with the resource-friendly butterfly unit takes
𝑂 ( d(𝑛/3)e + 2 · 𝑙𝑜𝑔4 (𝑛/2)) cycles due to the difference of the number of Montgomery multiplier
used. Also, two pipelined delays will add to the total latency for both architectures.
   Overall, the major advantages of the proposed NTT accelerators lie in: (i) flexibility to support
𝑛 = 256, 𝑛 = 512, and 𝑛 = 1,024 with Radix-4 (or like) complexities; (ii) applicability to different
parameter settings with respect to different PQC schemes, including Falcon, Dilithium, and HAWK.
One potential “overhead” for such benefits may be the additional control logic and datapaths required
within the accelerators. Nevertheless, compared with the gains we obtain from such architectural
innovations, these “extra limitations” are minor and negligible, as demonstrated in the next section.

5.2     Overall Performance
As shown in Tables 1–3, our proposed accelerators consistently demonstrate superior performance
across different PQC schemes, namely Falcon, Dilithium, and HAWK. Nevertheless, we still have
some interesting observations on the obtained performance data. First of all, the LUT and FF usage
decrease when 𝑛 goes from 512 to 1,024, because the accelerator operating at 𝑛 = 512 needs a
slight “overhead” for extra logic in Radix-2 determination and associated calculations as well as the
switch between Radix-2 and Radix-4. Second, due to the necessity for storing two sets of twiddle
factors for 𝑛 and 𝑛/2, the memory usage of the proposed ones is slightly high, and meanwhile,
the memory usage of 𝑛 = 512 is slightly higher than 𝑛 = 1,024. Lastly, although the overhead is
inevitable, the two proposed architectures still maintain high efficiency by utilizing relatively low
resources and operating at high frequency, and thus outperform the existing designs in the major
metric of EADP (as discussed in the following sections).

5.3     Comparison with the Existing Works for Falcon
We have listed the implementation results of the proposed and existing ones in Table 1 for 𝑛 = 512

the proposed NTT accelerator has at least 83.32% and 62.38% more efficiency in EADP than [19]
and [5] on Virtex-7. Meanwhile, it can achieve 47.05% and 80.68% lower EADP compared with
[32] and [30] on Zynq-7000. Considering the special case of comparing with [33], as shown in
Table 1, the proposed design achieved a 6.92% reduction in EADP on the Artix-7 platform. All these
comparisons undoubtedly prove that the proposed accelerators have superior performance when
implementing Falcon parameter settings.
   Note we noticed that Duong-Ngoc and Lee [7] proposed a Mixed-Radix Multi-path Delay
Feedback (MDF) NTT for Falcon, but this design did not report the data memory usage (which
is actually needed, see Figure 7 in [7]). However, we still included it in the comparison. As is
shown in Table 1, although it does not include any memory, the advantage in other resource usage
like LUT and DSP still leads to at least 20.82% reduction in EADP for 𝑛 = 512 and 40.26% for
𝑛 = 1,024. Meanwhile, we want to mention that there exist fundamental algorithmic differences
between the proposed designs and [7]. The work of Duong-Ngoc and Lee [7] utilizes a DIT NTT,
which requires a bit-reversed input ordering. In contrast, our approach is based on a DIF NTT,
which operates on naturally ordered input data. The order of sub-NTT varies based on the method,
and leads to different algorithms and architecture designs. Furthermore, while the Mixed-Radix
concept has been explored, the specific method combining our simplified butterfly operations
and derivations, which are unique to our article, has not been presented before. Moreover, on the
hardware architecture aspect, our hardware design and architecture are fundamentally different.
The work of Duong-Ngoc and Lee [7] employs a MDF architecture. This architecture uses a pipeline
of serially connected butterflies to process coefficients and avoids writing back to memory, which is
a key design choice. Our approach, however, uses an in-place write-back NTT architecture, where
the results of each stage are written back to memory. This design decision is critical for our overall
resource optimization.

5.6     Discussion and Future Work
While a direct quantitative comparison between FPGAs and other platforms like ASICs, GPUs, and
CPUs is challenging due to the lack of a universal metric—for instance, ASIC area is measured in
square millimeters while FPGA resources are measured in LUTs and FF (and others)—we believe
each platform has its own distinct advantages. Our work focuses on FPGAs, specifically the Artix-7,
aligns with the NIST recommendations [20] and provides a consistent benchmark against other
state-of-the-art implementations in our field. This allows for a clear and fair comparison.
   Overall, the proposed NTT acceleration strategy on the FPGA platform is highly efficient, not
only innovatively exploring a universal method for NTT computation under various parameter
settings but also fully utilizing the resources available on the FPGA devices. One prominent feature
of the proposed EMINEM (including both algorithm and accelerators) is that it can execute the NTT
computation process by achieving Radix-4 speed, even when 𝑛 is not a power-of-4. This makes it
applicable to various post-quantum lattice-based cryptographic schemes (high potential used as a
standard method in various NIST lattice-based schemes), especially after extending this strategy to
another NIST-standardized lattice-based scheme Kyber.
   Due to the aforementioned various factors, such as reconfigurability, parallel processing, and
flexibility, FPGA acceleration for computationally intensive algorithms, like the targeted NIST
PQC schemes, will become the mainstream research in the field. Following this trend, future work
may also focus on deploying the proposed NTT-based polynomial multiplication to construct
efficient PQC accelerators on FPGAs for various applications. Side-channel analysis and related
countermeasure design based on the proposed NTT method can be another primary direction.
Future research may also consider providing a more holistic performance analysis between different
platforms like ASICs, GPUs, and CPUs.

6     Conclusion
This article has presented a novel design framework for efficient Mixed-Radix NTT hardware
accelerators (EMINEM), covering three NIST lattice-based PQC schemes (Falcon, Dilithium, and
HAWK). Our contributions include a new Mixed-Radix NTT algorithm, a versatile hardware
accelerator design, two butterfly architectures with different performance focuses (achieved by
efficiently utilizing FPGA resources and novel datapath design), and detailed final implementations
and comparisons. The proposed design strategy surpasses the limitations of existing Radix-2
implementations for 512-length NTT designs, achieving superior performance and enabling the
deployment of Mixed-Radix NTT in NIST lattice-based PQC. Overall, the outcome of this article
will pave the way for further efficient and innovative implementations of NIST lattice-based PQC
schemes on the FPGA platforms. We hope this research will impact the ongoing PQC standardization
and initiate further follow-up work on PQC hardware implementation on FPGA platforms.
