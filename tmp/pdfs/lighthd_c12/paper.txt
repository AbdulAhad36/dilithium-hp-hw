Abstract—CRYSTALS-Dilithium serves as the foundation of
the NIST-standardised PQC digital signature scheme, and has
been declared as the ﬁrst recommended digital signature algo-
rithm. However, due to the computational complexity and intri-
cate processing ﬂow of CRYSTALS-Dilithium, two limitations
are shown in existing methods: its applicability on resource-
constrained devices is limited and the performance reported so
far remains relatively low. This paper presents a lightweight yet
high-performance hardware architecture that optimises the core
computational units of CRYSTALS-Dilithium. First, an iterative
dual-Keccak SHA-3 module is proposed, where two cores operate
with a 26-cycle offset to accelerate processing without compromis-
ing frequency. In addition, the rejection sampler is streamlined
by two compact registers for intermediate values and counters,
improving efﬁciency when consuming interleaved SHA-3 outputs.
Second, for small bit-width polynomials, we eliminate the ﬁrst
NTT stage via lookup tables and data regrouping, reducing
NTT cycles by 11.7% with little hardware overhead. Further
hardware savings are achieved by maximising IP core utilisation
and simplifying input multiplexers. Furthermore, a compact
scheduling strategy ensures that all intermediate storage ﬁts
within a single polynomial-sized memory block. On Xilinx Artix-7
FPGAs, the design reduces hardware overhead by 14.2% com-
pared with state-of-the-art lightweight implementations. Across
three security levels, KeyGen and Verify are 27.3% and 13.5%
faster, respectively, than high-performance prior designs. At level
5, the best-case Sign latency is only 120 µs.

I. INTRODUCTION
P
OST quantum cryptography (PQC) refers to public-key
cryptography based on complex mathematical problems
that are believed to remain secure against quantum adversaries.
Deployed public-key schemes such as RSA [1] and elliptic-
curve cryptography (ECC) [2] would be efﬁciently broken by
quantum computers due to Shor’s algorithm [3], whilst Grover’s
algorithm [4] reduces the effective security of symmetric prim-
itives and hash functions. In 2016, the US National Institute of
Standards and Technology (NIST) initiated the PQC standardis-
ation process, with 69 schemes submissions in the Round 1 [5].
After four rounds, NIST selected ﬁve schemes for standardis-
ation, i.e., CRYSTALS-Kyber [6], CRYSTALS-Dilithium [7],
SPHINCS+ [8], Falcon [9], and HQC [10]. In August 2024,
NIST published released Federal Information Processing Stan-
dards (FIPS: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) [11], FIPS 204 (ML-
DSA) [12], and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) [13]. The CRYSTALS-
Dilithium digital signature scheme, that makes the basis of the
FIPS 204, is widely regarded as the primary digital signature
algorithm for broad deployment, owing to its membership in
the CRYSTALS family of lattice-based schemes, relatively high
efﬁciency and simple implementation, and strong theoretical
foundations [14], [15].
NIST evaluates PQC candidates primarily on security and
performance, with particular attention to deployability on
lightweight platforms such as Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices.
Compared with certain instruction-set-based co-processor de-
signs [16], [17], [18], pure-hardware implementations can
deliver higher computational efﬁciency and throughput. For
CRYSTALS-Dilithium, prior hardware research falls broadly
into two lines: high-speed architectures [19], [20], [21], [22],
[23] and lightweight designs [14], [24], [25]. In the context
of lightweight architectures, Gupta et al. report one of the
most lightweight hardware architecture to date, with the en-
tire CRYSTALS-Dilithium implementation using only 13,975
LUTs and 6,875 FFs on Artix-7 [24], though it consumes 35
BRAMs and runs nearly 4× slower than state-of-the-art high-
performance architectures. Moreover, all existing architectures
operate at relatively low frequencies. For example, the design
in [14] and [21] executes at only 96.9 MHz and 119 MHz
on Artix-7, respectively, which signiﬁcantly lags behind other
standardized PQC implementations. A common contributing
factor is the widespread use of dual-Keccak hashing design
in CRYSTALS-Dilithium, where cascading two rounds of the

To address the slow speed and large hardware overhead
of current CRYSTALS-Dilithium hardware architectures, we
propose a high-speed and lightweight CRYSTALS-Dilithium
architecture. To the best of our knowledge, the proposed
CRYSTALS-Dilithium architecture is the fastest and most
lightweight FPGA-based hardware accelerator to date. We ﬁrst
adopt a novel iterative Keccak architecture that signiﬁcantly
reduces the overall area consumption while increasing the op-
erating frequency to 212 MHz, effectively realising the perfor-
mance potential of other components. Additionally, we further
optimise key modules, such as the rejection sampler and NTT,
to reduce both area and cycle count. A more compact scheduling
method is proposed to eliminate the need for additional poly-
nomial multiplication modules and memory. The main contri-
butions of this work are summarised as follows.
• An interleaved SHA-3 operation strategy is proposed to re-
duce the resource consumption and enhance overall oper-
ating frequency. Additionally, a sampler for the interleaved
SHA-3 output is introduced, which uses only two small
registers to sample two polynomials in real time.
• The calculation overhead of ﬁrst stage is completely elim-
inated through regrouping and lookup tables of small
bit-width vectors in number theoretic transform (NTT)
computation, reducing the cycle count of a single NTT
computation by 11.7%. In addition, merging part of the
operations with the DSP blocks and reducing the input
multiplexing logic is done to further reduce hardware over-
head in the polynomial module.
• A more compact scheduling method is proposed, process-
ing polynomial computations iteratively on a row-by-row
basis. This approach requires only a single polynomial-
sized memory for intermediate values throughout the en-
tire operation, eliminating the need for additional polyno-
mial multiplication modules.

CRYSTALS-Dilithium and NTT basics. Section III describes
the architecture, including BRAM-based memory, an inter-

sampler, and a lightweight small-bit-width polynomial module.
Section IV introduces a compact schedule for unifying the
three security levels. Section V presents implementation and
comparisons, and Section VI concludes.

B. NTT in CRYSTALS-Dilithium
Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) is widely used in lattice-
based cryptography to accelerate polynomial multiplication.
Compared with traditional algorithms such as Schoolbook al-
gorithms, NTT reduces the computational complexity from
O(n2) to O(n log n). Although NTT typically requires the
modulus q to be “NTT-friendly” primes, i.e., satisfying q ≡1
(mod 2n) so that a 2n-th root of unity exists, mainstream
lattice-based schemes such as Kyber and Dilithium are designed
with this consideration and select suitable moduli accordingly.

using parameters n = 256 and q = 8380417, where a 2n-th root

C. Related Work
Several pure-hardware implementations of CRYSTALS-
Dilithium have also been reported in the literature [14], [19],
[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]. Ricci et al. proposed the ﬁrst VHDL-
based CRYSTALS-Dilithium accelerator and showed substan-
tially higher performance on Xilinx Virtex UltraScale+ FPGAs
than HLS-based designs [19]. Beckwith et al. introduced a low-
latency hardware architecture targeting the third-round version
of CRYSTALS-Dilithium [20]. In 2022, Zhao et al. optimised
key internal modules of CRYSTALS-Dilithium, such as NTT,
SampleInBall, and Decompose, achieving a 1.4–4.5× reduction
in hardware cost while attaining a 4.4–1.7× speedup compared
to previous designs [14]. In 2023, Li et al. presented an architec-
ture based on multiple instruction parallel execution, reaching a
frequency of 300 MHz on UltraScale+ FPGAs, though the de-
sign still incurred considerable area overhead [22]. In the same
year, Gupta et al. proposed a lightweight CRYSTALS-Dilithium
accelerator consuming only 13,975 LUTs; however, it relied on
a larger amount of BRAM and exhibited signiﬁcantly lower
throughput than state-of-the-art designs [24]. In 2024, Zhao
et al. introduced a sparse-matrix-based 2-level rejection loop
design and reconstructed the polynomial module via LUTs,
improving Sign performance but incurring an additional 9
BRAMs [21]. Most recently, Cui et al. proposed a CRYSTALS-
Dilithium accelerator based on an improved Multi-Delay Com-
mutator (MDC) NTT, achieving a 26.4% to 29.6% speed-up
relative to state-of-the-art implementations, alongside a signiﬁ-
cant area increase [23]. Overall, current CRYSTALS-Dilithium
accelerators still leave substantial headroom for optimisation of
speed and area.
III. ARCHITECTURE OPTIMISATION PRINCIPLE
To overcome these limitations, we propose a lightweight
CRYSTALS-Dilithium architecture with three key enhance-
ments: (i) an iterative SHA-3 and sampler design that reduces
area whilst improving frequency through critical-path optimisa-
tion, (ii) polynomial-module optimisations that maximise DSP
utilisation and minimise input multiplexing, and (iii) elimina-
tion of the ﬁrst NTT stage for small bit-width polynomials. In
this section, we ﬁrst present the overall CRYSTALS-Dilithium
hardware architecture, then discuss its submodules in detail,
including the memory conﬁguration, SHA-3, sampling, and
polynomial modules. We describe only those subfunctions that
we speciﬁcally optimise in this work.
A. Overall Architecture
The overall CRYSTALS-Dilithium architecture is shown in
Fig. 1. The proposed architecture consists of two parts: the
signer-side and the veriﬁer-side. The signer-side handles Key-
Gen and Sign phase, while the veriﬁer side only processes
the Verify phase. Both sides support switching among the three
security levels. At the start of the architecture, the 2-bit security-
level signal is used to select the corresponding ﬁxed parame-
ters, which remain unchanged throughout the current execution.

In terms of memory, the proposed architecture relies on four
BRAM blocks, each sized 92 × 1024, to buffer polynomial
results, intermediate data for processing, and the sampling unit
outputs; the detailed organisation is given in Section III-B.
Polynomial computations are performed by an NTT core and a
small Pre-NTT unit. The NTT module supports NTT, Inverse-
NTT (INTT) and point-wise multiplication (PWM). For small
bit-width polynomials, the ﬁrst NTT stage is handled by the Pre-
NTT through LUT-based regrouping, which reduces cycles and
input multiplexing. To achieve a lightweight architecture, we
do not incorporate additional polynomial and memory units for
pipelined 2-level rejection loops. Although such 2-level rejec-
tion loops can improve throughput, the sparse-polynomial unit
in [21] incurs an overhead of 2,778 LUTs and 9 BRAMs, which
is prohibitive on resource-constrained devices. In addition, the
architecture includes a SHA-3 module with two Keccak cores
[26] to enhance computational efﬁciency. The sampling module
incorporates four types of samplers, two rejection sampler and
SamplingMask (used to generate secret key in Sign phase), and
a distrubted-RAM based Sample in Ball (SIB). The processing
ﬂow from the NTT computation results to the ﬁnal output
is categorised as the Process module. This module includes
the Decompose×4 (capable of processing four sets of data
simultaneously), the Check Normal Bounds (CNBs), the Make
Hint (MHint), and the Power-2-Round (P2R) module. All bit-
packing (BitPkg)-related modules have been integrated, with a
selector enabling ﬂexible processing based on different poly-
nomial types and security levels. The controller utilizes a ﬁnite
state machine (FSM) to manage key modules such as SHA-3,
NTT, and BRAM. To enhance ﬂexibility, separate FSMs are
used for the two BRAM ports, allowing independent control.

The design uses BRAM as the primary on-chip memory.

security level 5. BRAM4 is used as a buffer for matrix A during
the computation of w = A ◦y and is later utilised for storing
z and partial intermediate results during boundary checking in
Sign phase.
C. Interleaved SHA-3 and Sampling Modules
In CRYSTALS-Dilithium, SHAKE256 from the SHA-3 fam-
ily is used for sampling input data and computing hash val-
ues. Fig. 3(a) shows the baseline SHA-3/Keccak module. With
a single Keccak core and a 64-bit input/ output, one oper-
ation proceeds through absorb, Keccak-f[1600] permutation,
and squeeze in 25, 24, and 25 cycles, respectively. Given high
sampling demand of CRYSTALS-Dilithium, most implemen-
tations adopt two Keccak cores, typically in either a cascaded
[14], [22] or a parallel [23]. In the cascaded scheme, two Kec-
cak cores are cascaded as shown in Fig. 3(c), completing two
rounds of Keccak-f[1600] permutation within a single cycle,

independently, keeping the critical path short, similar to a single
core, but producing multiple hash streams concurrently; this
raises area pressure due to extra buffering and requires sampler
coordination [23].
To address these problems, in the proposed architecture,
a novel scheduling method is ﬁrst introduced for the dual-
Keccak-based SHA-3 module. To prevent frequency degrada-
tion caused by cascade Keccak cores, the two Keccak cores
operate independently with a ﬁxed 26-cycle offset. As shown
in Fig. 3(d), while the ﬁrst Keccak core in Keccak permutation
phase, the second Keccak core simultaneously enters the absorb
phase, and their squeeze phases interleaved. The two SHA-3
instances operate independently, while within each SHA-3 core
the absorb and squeeze phases are executed sequentially and
share the same Keccak core datapath. Since most SHA-3 calls
in CRYSTALS-Dilithium serve sampling, assuming each hash
sampling requires four squeeze operations and the bit width of
SHA-3 input/ output being 64 bits, the cycle count of single
sampling under the cascaded method is 25 + 12 + 25 + (12 +
25) × 4 = 210. By contrast, the interleaved method completes
two samplings within just 25 + (24 + 25) × 5 + 26 = 296

on their validity, allowing the sampler to process a 64-bit SHA-3
result in a single cycle. In addition, a sampling counter triggers
an output whenever the number of accepted results exceeds 15.
The sampling of matrix A follows a similar approach. Every
three 64-bit SHA-3 outputs are grouped together, generating
eight sampled values over three cycles, with 2, 3, and 3 data
blocks processed per cycle, respectively. Valid coefﬁcients are
accumulated through a selector, and output whenever four valid
samples have been gathered. This lane-parallel design keeps the
sampler aligned with the interleaved SHA-3 design and avoids
intermediate buffering.
Although the proposed interleaved dual-Keccak SHA-3 ar-
chitecture addresses the low frequency and reduces the need to
buffer SHA-3 outputs, its interleaved outputs introduce addi-
tional challenges for the sampling module. After each sampling,
the internal Buffer register and the counter (cnt) may still hold
intermediate data associated with the current polynomial sam-

proper state isolation between polynomial samplings, the use

output. It should be noted that Instantiating two samplers is
inefﬁcient, as rejection sampling already requires signiﬁcant

D. NTT for Small Bit-Width Polynomials
In previous research, the Multi-path Delay Commutator-NTT
(MDC-NTT) pipeline architecture [27] has been widely adopted
in hardware architectures of CRYSTALS-Dilithium [14], [21],
[23]. This type of NTT provides better compatibility between
output throughput and memory units. However, it requires con-
tinuous input of NTT/INTT data to avoid the long pipeline
startup latency, which limits the ﬂexibility of scheduling. In the
proposed architecture, we utilise a iterative NTT design, which
consists of 4 butterﬂy units (BUs) and FIFO-based memory,
similar to [28]. The FIFO bit width is set to 92 bits, with bit
depths of 32, 32, and 16, respectively. In addition, the proposed
NTT design supports PWM computation and allows merging of
single polynomial addition/subtraction operations with PWM
computation.
Here, we further introduce two optimisation strategies to
maximise the utilisation of available IP resources and to lower
overall hardware cost. In addition, by leveraging a small amount
of look-up tables and data regrouping, a portion of the ﬁrst NTT
stage is bypassed.

2) Minimise Input Multiplexing Logic: In the polynomial
architecture, besides the arithmetic units such as multipliers,
reducers, and adders used for computation, a signiﬁcant amount
of on-chip resources is also consumed by the data selection
logic for port registers of instantiated modules. For example,
in previous NTT architectures, BU typically adopts a ﬂoating
submodule design, in which each BU contains a complete set
of multipliers, adders, and subtractors. Depending on whether
the operation is NTT or INTT, different input paths are se-
lected via multiplexers to accommodate varying computational
patterns. However, this approach introduces considerable area
overhead in input selection. For example, a 23-bit adder nomi-
nally requires about 23 LUTs, whereas the associated 2:1 input
selection can add at least a further 46 LUTs (one 2:1 multi-
plexer per bit on each operand), often exceeding the cost of the
adder itself.
To address this, we adopt a forward data-path in which each
BU contains only forward paths and minimises input selection
across internal blocks. In the proposed DSP conﬁguration for
NTT, the NTT and INTT operations are implemented using the
forms A × B + C and (D −A) × B + C, respectively. This
keeps that the inputs to the A and B ports of the DSP identical
in NTT and INTT, thereby eliminating operand switching and
saving at least 46 LUTs. Moreover, For DSP2, the C port
always sources the output of DSP1 in both modes, which
likewise removes input multiplexing. A similar optimisation is
applied at the BU outputs. According to Eq. (5) and Eq. (8),
the modular reduction unit outputs x and yi, respectively. Since
results are reassembled before entering the FIFO, we order the
BU outputs as (x, y) in NTT and (yi, xi) in INTT, avoiding
an output selector altogether. In the proposed architecture, each
BU module occupies only 252 LUTs and 2 DSPs, among which
the modular reduction unit alone consumes 96 LUTs. In con-
trast, in [25], only modular multiplier within the polynomial
module consumes 260 LUTs and 2 DSP slices.
In addition, the top-level control logic of the polynomial
module incorporates a signal generator and three decoders,
i.e., De_BU, De_TW and De_FIFO to further reduce the area
overhead caused by input selection logic. The signal generator
is responsible for producing twiddle factor control signals and
FIFO read/write control signals. In both NTT and INTT modes,
it ﬁrst generates the FIFO read control and twiddle factor se-
lection signals, and then produces other control signals through
multiple shifters, aiming to minimise area consumption. The
BU decoder (De_BU) is used to select the data sources for
both the BU input and the twiddle factor. Its inputs include
the control signals from the NTT state machine and the FIFO
output control signals. For example, in the proposed architec-
ture, single-bit of BU input receives input bits from up to four
FIFO data blocks and the top-level NTT input. In addition to the
data inputs, the control logic for selecting the appropriate data
involves multiple control signals, including a 4-bit state signal,
a 2-bit NTT/INTT/PWM mode selector, a 1-bit start signal, and
a 1-bit input interleaving signal, resulting in a total of 8 control
bits. For each BU, a single output bit depends on 12 to 14
input bits, depending on whether the data source contributes
4, 5, or 6 bits. A direct implementation would therefore require
substantial combinational logic. We observe that, for all eight
BU inputs, the selection never involves more than six data bits.
This allows each BU input to be controlled by a compact 3-
bit selector. By exploiting the regularity of the BU inputs in

3) Small Bit-width Polynomials-Based Optimisation: No-
tably, the range of secret key sampling results in CRYSTALS-
Dilithium is relatively small. In security levels 2 and 5, the co-
efﬁcient range of the secret key polynomial during the KeyGen
phase is [−2, 2], while in security level 3, the range is [−4, 4].
As a result, the number of possible secret key polynomial coef-
ﬁcients is limited: there are 5 possible values in security levels
2 and 5, and 9 possible values in security level 3. This allows
for the precomputation and storage of all potential ﬁrst-stage
outputs in a lookup table (LUT), enabling the ﬁrst stage of NTT
computation to be skipped. For the butterﬂy unit (BU), when the
input multiplied by the twiddle factor is zero, there is no need
to precompute the result. Therefore, for a coefﬁcient range of
[−2, 2], the BU produces 20 possible outputs (4 × 5), whereas
expanding the range to [−4, 4] increases the number of stored
results to 72 (8 × 9).
However, eliminating the ﬁrst stage of the NTT solely
through LUT substitution remains challenging in practice. This
is because, in FIFO-based NTT designs, when the inputs follow
the pairing sequence deﬁned by the NTT (e.g., (0, 128)), the
output of the BU is ﬁrst stored in a FIFO until its corresponding
pair (e.g., (64, 192)) is processed, in order to generate the input
pair (0, 64) for the second stage. Therefore, if the inputs are
still fed into the NTT module following the original pairing
pattern ((0,128), (1,129). . .), then the outputs obtained from the
LUT must still be reordered via the FIFO. In this case, using
LUT only reduces the pipeline startup latency by eliminating the
initial computation cycles of the ﬁrst-stage BUs. To address this
issue, if the LUT module is capable of directly outputting data
in the pairing order required by the second stage of the NTT,
the ﬁrst stage can be effectively eliminated. In the proposed
architecture, this is achieved by regrouping the small bit-width
polynomial coefﬁcients after the LUT generation phase, such
that their output naturally conforms to the second-stage input
pairing pattern. This enables, for the ﬁrst time, the NTT com-
putation in CRYSTALS-Dilithium hardware designs to begin
directly from the second stage. This regrouping operation is
performed on-the-ﬂy during sampling and has no impact on the
NTT cycle count.
In CRYSTALS-Dilithium, the data at order 0 in the second
stage originates from the ﬁrst stage computation result of pair-
ing (0, 128), while the data at order 64 in second stage comes
from the ﬁrst-stage result of pairing (64, 196). In a LUT-based
NTT design, the output at index 0 is generated in the ﬁrst cycle,
whereas the value at index 64 appears only after sixteen cycles,
so the ﬁrst NTT stage cannot be eliminated. In addition, a dual-
port BRAM has only two access ports, and the indices (0, 128,
64, and 192) map to four distinct memory locations that cannot
be read in one cycle. To overcome this limitation, the sampling
results are regrouped before entering the LUT so that the re-
quired inputs can be obtained using only the two BRAM ports.
Fig. 5 shows the process of data regrouping in the proposed
design. The module shifts 16 sampled data (64-bits each in
total) by four cycles, then regroups data blocks before storing
them separately. For example, in the ﬁrst cycle of regrouping,
data blocks with order (64, . . . , 71) is merged with (0, . . . , 7)
and stored at position 0 in BRAM (the ﬁrst address of the
current NTT input), while data blocks with order (72, . . . , 79)
is combined with (8, . . . , 15) and stored at position 1. Sim-
ilarly, data blocks with order (128, . . . , 135) is merged with
(192, . . . , 199) and stored at position 8. In the proposed design,
each pair of data blocks fetched from BRAM in a single access
is sufﬁcient to provide all the necessary inputs for a second-
stage NTT butterﬂy computation. For example, upon initiating
the NTT, the BRAM reads from addresses 0 and 8, retrieving
polynomial coefﬁcients with indices (0, . . . , 7, 64, . . . , 71) and
(128, . . . , 135, 192, . . . , 199), respectively. From these, the in-
put set (0, 64, 128, 192) can be directly selected and routed
through LUT to supply the necessary operands for the second-
stage computation as shown in Fig. 6(a). In each cycle of the
second-stage computation, only one of the paired outputs from
the ﬁrst stage is required. This implies that, during the NTT
input phase, each memory address must be accessed twice in
order to obtain both outputs of input paires in ﬁrst stage for the
second-stage pairing. As shown in Fig. 6(b), skipping the ﬁrst
NTT stage leaves only seven stages, each requiring 32 cycles.
In addition, the LUT introduces two extra pipeline-initialization
cycles for data preparation, while its own computation com-
pletes in a single cycle. With this approach, the total NTT com-
putation time is reduced to 235 cycles, resulting in an 11.7%
speed improvement. It is important to note that the additional
hardware introduced by this approach remains within accept-
able limits. The regrouping module requires only 104 LUTs,
while the LUT overhead is determined by the number of BUs in
the current polynomial module. Each BU is allocated 84 LUTs
for data selection purposes. This optimisation is applicable not
only to CRYSTALS-Dilithium but also to other lattice-based
cryptographic schemes that employ small-bit-width NTTs, such
as CRYSTALS-Kyber.

V. IMPLEMENTATION RESULTS AND COMPARISON
In this section, we report the resource utilisation and end-to-
end performance of the proposed CRYSTALS-Dilithium archi-
tecture, and compare it with state-of-the-art designs. The design
was simulated, synthesised and implemented on an Artix-7
(XC7A200T) device using AMD Xilinx Vivado 2020.1 Design
Suite. All secret-key dependent operations, including rejection
sampling and NTT-based polynomial arithmetic, are imple-
mented in constant time. The sampler always processes a ﬁxed
number of SHA-3 outputs and the polynomial multiplication
and Pre-NTT modules use access patterns determined only by
public parameters, so for a given security level the cycle count
is independent of the secret key and sampled values, providing
inherent resistance to timing attacks.
A. Resource Utilisation
Table II summarises the resource utilisation of the proposed
architecture. The signer-side consumes 20,623 LUTs, 13,854
FFs, 8 DSPs and 12 BRAMs in total. The SHA-3 block con-
taining two Keccak cores occupies 6,019 LUTs and 3,220 FFs
in total. Speciﬁcally, the two Keccak cores use 3,150 and 2,869
LUTs, respectively, and each consumes 1,610 FFs. Compared
with [14] (15,784 LUTs) and [21] (8,110 LUTs), this repre-
sents a reduction of approximately 2.6× and 1.3×, respectively,
whilst maintaining comparable computational speed. Although
the SHA-3 block employs an interleaved-output scheme that
increases sampler control complexity, the complete sampling
module (Table II) still uses only 4,035 LUTs including different
samplers required by CRYSTALS-Dilithium, which is compa-
rable to the sampler in [21]. The NTT core (polynomial mul-
tiplication) requires 2,601 LUTs, 2,730 FFs and 8 DSPs, and
supports NTT, INTT, PWM and second-stage startup. The Pre-
NTT module includes regrouping and LUT modules, consum-
ing 104 LUTs and 336 LUTs respectively. The on-chip memory
footprint is limited to 12 BRAMs. Post-implementation power
for the signer-side is estimated at 0.879 W by Vivado.
B. Overall Performance and Comparison
Table III compares area and timing with state-of-the-art de-
signs. Several works accelerate the Sign rejection loop by
adding dedicated compute and memory; for example, [21] pro-
visions an extra 2,778 LUTs, 615 FFs and 9 BRAMs for the
z and h stages to shorten runtime. To remain lightweight, our
design does not include the two-level rejection loop or these
additional resources. Moreover, our optimisation strategies do
not conﬂict with the 2-level rejection loop in [21]. If necessary,
the proposed architecture can also increase these additional

case and average signing performance, the comparison of Sign

the advantage of the proposed architecture.
Direct area comparison is difﬁcult because designs employ
different optimisation strategies and make varying use of LUTs,
FFs, DSPs and BRAMs. Following [30], we therefore report
the equivalent number of slices (ENS) by uniformly converting
resources: every four LUTs or eight FFs count as one slice, one
DSP counts as 100 slices, and one BRAM counts as 200 slices.
ENS ﬁgures for all architectures are listed in Table III. The
proposed design uses only 10,088 ENS, making it among the
most lightweight CRYSTALS-Dilithium accelerators reported
to date. Relative to the previously most compact design [24], our
architecture reduces ENS by 14.2% whilst supporting all three
security levels (whereas [24] targets only level 5). For a fairer
speed comparison, we also synthesised our design on a speed-
grade -2 device, achieving 178.6 MHz. At the same speed grade,
ourlatencies in security level 5 are 59.4 s, 143.3 s and 69.4 s for
KeyGen, Sign and Verify, respectively, which correspond to
6.5×, 4.9× and 6.0× improvements over [24]. Compared with
[14], our design reduces overall area by 15.9%. At the lowest
security level, KeyGen latency in cycles is comparable (4,144
cycles), while Sign and Verify require more cycles because the
iterative SHA-3 behaves as a single-core SHA-3 outside the
sampling phases. Nevertheless, the iterative SHA-3 shortens the
critical path and raises frequency, yielding signiﬁcant end-to-
end speed-ups: at the lowest level, KeyGen, Sign and Verify
improve by 54.5%, 33.1% and 43.2%, respectively; at level 5,
the corresponding gains are 44.7%, 26.2% and 37.0%. Design
in [14] did not share the speed grade level of its FPGA. Nev-
ertheless, even under the assumption of a -2 speed level, the
proposed architecture still demonstrates superior performance
in speed.

VI. CONCLUSION
In
this
paper,
we
propose
the
most
lightweight
CRYSTALS-Dilithium hardware architecture to date. First,
an iterative dual-Keccak SHA-3 architecture is presented,
where two Keccak cores operate independently with a 26-cycle
offset, producing interleaved hash outputs. This ensures
a uniﬁed critical path with the NTT and other modules
while reducing resource overhead from cascading Keccak
cores and input/output pipelining. Additionally, the rejection
sampler is optimised by introducing two small registers to
store intermediate values and counters, reducing resource
consumption, to accommodate the interleaved SHA-3 output.
Second, lookup tables and a data regrouping method are
utilised in the architecture to fully eliminate the ﬁrst stage
of NTT computation for low-bit-width secret polynomials
in CRYSTALS-Dilithium, achieving an 11.7% reduction
in NTT computation cycles. In terms of scheduling, a
more compact method is employed to split and iteratively
process polynomial computations on a row-by-row basis. The
intermediate memory required for all polynomial computations
is only the same amount of space as a single polynomial.
Experimental
results
show
that
compared
to
the
most
lightweight CRYSTALS-Dilithium hardware architecture, the
proposed design reduces hardware area by 14.2%. Additionally,
at all three security levels, the KeyGen and Verify phases
achieve speed improvements of 27.3% and 13.5%, respectively.
Speciﬁcally, at security level 5, the best-case Sign phase takes
only 120.3 s, compared to 126 s in state-of-the-art designs.
The architecture meets the performance requirements of
resource-constrained environments while enabling further
speed improvements through the integration of additional
polynomial computation modules and a 2-level rejection loop.
